Diplomatic Crisis Decision Lab

Fictional cyber crisis inspired by public allegations. No allegation in this exercise is treated as proven. Four rounds of strategic decisions, evidence assessment, and institutional design.

Choose your delegation. Each decision must be defended from its institutional perspective.

Read the six role mandates
  1. United States: Avoid premature attribution; protect allies and technical credibility.
  2. China: Demand recognition of security concerns and independent verification.
  3. UN facilitator: Prevent escalation; propose consent-based confidence-building measures.
  4. Regional partners: Protect critical infrastructure and avoid forced alignment.
  5. Private cyber firm: Protect proprietary methods while sharing verifiable indicators.
  6. Technical standards body: Restore trusted time synchronization without adjudicating blame.

Final seminar debrief

Defend your diplomatic architecture

  1. Which decision was most defensible under realism, and which under constructivism or pluralism?
  2. Identify one instance of vertical, horizontal, and functional multiplicity.
  3. Who had technical competence without formal authority? Who had formal authority without operational control?
  4. Where did venue shopping help coordination, and where did it risk fragmentation?
  5. Propose a verifiable agreement with an attribution standard, oversight body, and 30-day review.

Your decision record — copy and submit as a short policy memo.
